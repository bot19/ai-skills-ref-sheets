What DNS is

DNS stands for Domain Name System. It's the internet's address book.

Computers find each other by number. People remember names. DNS is the system that turns one into the other, and it's spread across millions of machines rather than kept in one place.

DNS isn't one thing. It's a set of pieces working together: the names themselves, the companies that keep the lists, the servers that answer questions, and the copies that get cached along the way. Each has its own section on this sheet.

google.com → 142.250.72.14  — that swap, done a few trillion times a day

Part 1 · Buying a domain (example.com)

Names are read backwards

Computers find each other by number — an IP address like 142.250.72.14. A domain name is a label pointing at one.

Names are read right to left, widest part first. Every dot is one step down a tree.

www  .  google  .  com  .
 │        │         │   └─ the root
 │        │         └─ top level
 │        └─ the bit you pay for
 └─ yours to invent, as many as you like

There are a few hundred top-level domains (.com, .org, .au). You rent one name inside one of them. Everything to the left of it is yours to create at no cost: blog.example.com, api.example.com.

What about example.com.au?

It's an extra layer, and a completely separate branch. .com.au has nothing to do with .com — they just reuse the same word, like two different folders each containing a subfolder called docs.

Only two things change: your signpost sits in .com.au rather than .com, and the registry is auDA rather than Verisign. Zone files, records, nameservers and caching all behave exactly as described elsewhere.

It can go deeper still. In jobs.careers.vic.gov.au, .au belongs to auDA and .gov is handed to the Digital Transformation Agency. Same idea, repeated.

Two practical differences. auDA has eligibility rules that .com doesn't — you generally need an Australian presence, usually an ABN. And your registrar has to be accredited for .au specifically; not all of them are.

Two zone files can exist. Only one is read.

Say you buy example.com at Namecheap, then build your site and host it on Cloudflare. During setup Cloudflare tells you to change your nameservers to ada.ns.cloudflare.com. Hosts ask for this so they can manage your DNS for you, switch on HTTPS automatically, and route visitors through their own network.

So you paste those names into Namecheap's nameserver box and save. You now have two zone files, both real, both editable: the one Namecheap made when you bought the name, and the one Cloudflare made when you signed up.

Part 2 · From google.com to an address

Your computer asks one server, not many

Your browser sends one question to one machine — a resolver, run by your internet provider, or a public one like 1.1.1.1. It does the whole hunt and comes back with a single answer.

Your computer never talks to the servers on the left.

It happens in that order, not all at once. The resolver can't ask the .com servers until the root tells it who they are, and can't ask ns1.google.com until .com names it. Each reply is what makes the next question askable.

What each level actually stores

The root reads the name right to left and only looks at the last part. Its whole list is the top-level domains — who runs .com, who runs .org. It has never heard of "google". The .com servers hold one line per .com domain, naming its nameservers.

Reception: "Don't know Sarah, but she's on floor 4."
Floor 4: "Don't know her either. Try the design team."

One name, different answers. Big sites reply with a different address depending on where you're asking from, so you get sent to a machine near you. Two people, two answers, both correct.

google.com from Sydney → 142.250.66.238
google.com from London → 142.250.200.14

Each level knows only who to ask next. That middle list is the registry from Part 1: the signpost you rent is one line in it, and this is where it gets read.

Part 3 · Why it's fast, and why changes take time

What happens to a saved answer

10:00

First person asks

Memory is empty, so the resolver does the full walk: root, then .com, then your nameservers.

slow · ~200ms
→

10:00 – 11:00

Everyone else asks

Handed the saved copy straight from memory. No walk at all, however many people ask.

instant
→

11:00

The hour is up

The copy is thrown away, and that is all that happens. Nothing is fetched — the resolver just has nothing to hand out.

→

11:07

Next person asks

Full walk again, and this time it reads whatever your zone says now. Your new address. Saved for another hour.

slow · ~200ms

That hour is the record's TTL — a number you set yourself, on each record. One hour is just this example; Google's A record is 300 seconds.

Every resolver has its own clock

The same four steps, plus a second resolver that happened to start its hour 25 minutes later.

"Propagation" isn't really a thing. Nothing gets pushed out across the internet. You're waiting for other people's copies to run out the clock. Drop the TTL the day before a change and the wait shrinks from an hour to minutes.
